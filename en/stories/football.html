What’s the difference between a tight end and a receiver?

Turns out this is the hardest question in NFL football.

Well, maybe not, but it was the trickiest question of a project I worked on.

I helped a football analytics company automate their data labelling process. For every play, in every game, all season long, the data labeling team paused the recording and identified the players on the field and what position they played. Sounds easy: check the team roster, scan the numbers on the jerseys, and done. Not quite. There are times when players are in a different position for a play, or you can’t see the jersey clearly. The annotation manual suggests identifying players using other features than their uniform, like the color of their shoelaces for example. Not so easy.

Life got better when the NFL started tracking the players. Now, each player’s location on the field is recorded multiple times per second. My project was to take the tracking data, and the guidebook and create an algorithm to label all the players, without looking at their shoelaces.

Once the quarterback was identified, most of the offensive positions were straightforward: the center directly in front, guards and tackles on either side. Wide receivers at the outer edges. For the defensive positions, the guide book described very clear regions relative to the offensive players and depth from the line of scrimmage. Depths are hard to estimate on the TV, easy when you’ve got the exact positions of everyone.

Now, back to the tight end: not part of the offensive line, but not a wide receiver either. According to the guidebook, a tight end has one hand on the ground, a wide receiver has no hands on the ground. Easy enough when you can see the players. The tracking data doesn’t include hand positions though. Fortunately, we had access to a full year’s worth of labeled data. Some quick analysis helped identify a threshold distance. Players within this distance of the offensive linesmen were tight ends, outside wide receivers. Not a perfect solution, but the client identified a threshold they were happy with.

In the end, the algorithm did very well. It took many iterations, comparisons to the existing labels, and discussions with the client to identify some of the nuances that were tripping us up. I made a visualization tool that helped immensely, and we even found some instances where the existing labels were incorrect. By the time the next football season started, the client had a working solution that they trusted.

I had more fun on this project than I initially thought I would. I’m not really into sports, and (still) have never watched a football game. It was a bit of a running joke at the beginning, but the project really wasn’t about football. The project was about understanding the data, extracting the important information from it, and delivering a working solution. That I can do.
