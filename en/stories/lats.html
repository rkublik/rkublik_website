Don't wait for me, I'll catch up

Simulations of electrical activity in nerve cells rely on solving large systems of non-linear differential equations thousands of times for each second of simulated time. These simulations can help us better understand how parts of the brain work, but they are very time consuming and computationally expensive.

There are two main components to the computational cost: the size of the physical domain which corresponds to how many equations are in the system; and how many times we need to solve the equations. There is a parameter referred to as stepsize that controls the second of these: the smaller the stepsize, the more times we need to solve the system of equations. In general, a system that evolves quickly (has a high activity level) requires a small time step to accurately capture the changes.

In the most naive approach, the stepsize is constant for the entire simulation, and is determined based on the highest activity level expected at any time during the simulation. This is simple to implement, but leads to extra work if the system evolves slowly except during a short time within the simulation.

A more refined approach is to use an adaptive time step. At each update, the stepsize selected is based on the fastest evolution at that instant, anywhere in the physical domain. This can reduce the computational cost, and isn't much more complicated to implement than the first method. In fact, like the first method, there are standard packages available in most computational libraries to do this.

Sometimes in neural simulations the system of interest is very large, but there are only a small number of regions with high activity at any time. Most of the system could be simulated with a large stepsize, but since there is always some region with high activity, the simulation needs to select a small stepsize. As a graduate student, I developed an algorithm for these cases.

I developed an algorithm for locally adaptive time stepping (LATS). Within this scheme, the spatial domain is split into subdomains, and each subdomain is updated with an adaptive time step most appropriate for the local level of activity. Each subdomain evolves at its own pace, and waits for its neighbors to catch up. The challenge is in maintaining accurate flow of information and stability of the solution.

Through the application of domain decomposition techniques, I was able to computationally connect the subdomains through boundary conditions obtained through a conservation of flux. To address the stability concerns, I replaced the time stepping scheme that had been used for neuroscience simulations since the 1960s with a method that provides better stability and proved better suited to the LATS algorithm.

Evaluating the LATS algorithm is not as simple as stating an X% reduction in computational time. The underlying numerical scheme is comparable to the standard approach, but the major benefit of the LATS method is that the computational cost scales with the level of activity in the system, rather than the physical size of the domain. Thus in situations where there is sparse activity in a large computational domain, the LATS method provides a significant reduction in computational cost by focusing computational resources where they are most needed. The LATS method was developed within the context of computational neuroscience, but is applicable to any system with sparse activity.
